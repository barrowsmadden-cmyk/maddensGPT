# I. Introduction to Inheritance Law

## A. The Probate Process (BGL 3-21)

### 1. Terminology

**Testate administration**
- A person dying testate is the testator or testatrix
- Devises real property to devisees
- Bequeaths personal property (bequests) to legatees
- Modern terminology: gifts in a will can be called devises, bequests, or gifts

**Intestate administration**
- A person dying without a will that completely disposes of the estate is fully or partially intestate and is called a decedent
- Real property descends to heirs
- Personal property is distributed to next of kin, or distributees
- Inheritance is described in shares (fractions, not decimals)

### 2. Probate vs. Nonprobate Property

**Probate property** passes through a will or through intestacy
- It is property that is "orphaned" at the time of death, so it must go through either a will or intestacy

**Nonprobate property** passes through an *instrument other than a will that became effective **before** death*
- Common types:
  - Joint tenancy property
  - Life insurance
  - Trust interests
  - Payable-on-death contracts/stocks/accounts

### 3. Probate ("to prove")

Probate is a specialized court and a process where wills are proved to be valid, or where it is determined how the estate will be distributed under the rules of intestacy.
- Probate can only be opened in the jurisdiction where the decedent was domiciled

**Functions of probate**
- Protect creditors by requiring notice and payment of debts
- Pay taxes
- Provide evidence of transfer of title to new owners
- Distribute property to intended recipients

**Steps in probate**
1. Someone offers the death certificate + the will for probate, or an affidavit of no will
2. Statute of limitations to start probate, and probate starts the limitations period for creditors
3. Proponent (the person who offered the will) asks the court to appoint a personal representative (PR)
   - If named in the will, the PR is called the **executor**
   - If not named, the PR is called the **administrator**
     - The statutory order of priority for relatives to serve as administrator differs by jurisdiction
4. Court designates someone as PR and issues **letters testamentary** or **letters of administration** (both usually just court orders appointing the PR and giving them legal authority to manage the estate)
5. PR puts up a bond and gets paid fees; the PR can be sued for wrongdoing

# II. Wills

### Overview: Requirements of a Valid Will

A valid will must have:
1. Testamentary capacity
2. Testamentary intent
3. Formality of execution

## A. Mental Capacity (BGL 45-63)

### 1. Requirements
1. At least 18 years old to write a will
2. A sound mind (testamentary capacity)

### 2. What Is a Sound Mind?
- The purpose of requiring a sound mind is to protect family members from disinheritance
- **Restatement Test**
  1. Knows the nature and extent of one's property
  2. Knows the natural objects of one's bounty
     - Do they know where their property would usually go without a will? Typically: do they understand their property usually goes to a spouse and children?
     - This gets harder when the person has more complicated heirs (no children, no spouse, etc.)
  3. Understands the pattern of disposition undertaken
  4. Understands the relationship between all these elements: the act provides an orderly disposition

### 3. Cases

**Wilson v. Lane (Ga. 2005) (Modern)**
- Kathy Lane offers the 1997 will of Jewel Greer for probate; Floyd files a "caveat"
- *Why does Floyd have standing?*
  - He was a "natural object" who would benefit if the will were held invalid
  - Most often, the people with standing to challenge a will are intestate heirs
- *Test applied:* A person has testamentary capacity if she has sufficient intellect to enable her to have a decided and rational desire as to the disposition of her property
- *Burden of proof:*
  - Propounders must make out a prima facie case to establish a presumption of capacity
  - Caveators must rebut the presumption of capacity in order to invalidate the will
- *If the testator is proven to lack capacity:* the will must be denied probate, regardless of its terms

**Wright (Traditional)**
- Eccentric behavior: testator gives relatives fish "poked in kerosene", offers to buy things not for sale, tells people he sent them turkeys as gifts, collects things from the rubbish

**Traditional vs. Modern approach**
- Traditional (*Wright*): eccentric behavior
- Modern (*Wilson*): Alzheimer's or some other diagnosed disease (still a bit of the traditional approach, but courts are more inclined to attribute certain behaviors to disease)

### 4. Classic Degrees of Capacity
In descending order of the capacity needed to do the act:
1. Make a contract / make a gift
2. Avoid a conservatorship
3. Make a will
4. Marry

- *Why is higher capacity needed to contract than to make a will?*
  - The law is more wary of interfering with the ability to choose what happens to your property and to marry; these are natural human rights (we value property ownership and the ability to marry)

### 5. Insane Delusion
- A mistaken belief of fact held despite all evidence to the contrary
- To invalidate a will, you must show the delusion **caused** the will or part of the will
  - The will can be partially or totally invalidated
- If the court can see that the dispositive provisions were or might have been caused or affected by the delusion, the instrument is not valid
- **Key questions**
  1. Was it a delusion?
  2. Was it insane?
  3. Did it actually cause the testator to make that will?

**In re Strittmater's Estate (N.J. 1947)** (BGL p. 55)
- Testator said she hated all men
- Her bequest seemed consistent with that hatred; she left her money to a feminist radical group

**Matter of Honigman (N.Y. 1960)** (BGL p. 57)
- To invalidate the disinheritance of his wife, the challenger had to show:
  - No other reasonable person would believe his wife was cheating; and
  - He disinherited her because of that insane delusion

**Burden of proof**
- *Honigman:* the court "split the burden." The wife had to show evidence that there was a delusion and that it caused the will; then the proponents had to disprove either of those
- *Modern view:* if the will appears valid on its face (nothing stands out as wrong), the burden is on the challenger to prove lack of capacity
- It's not always clear, but you need to be able to answer the question of who bears the burden

## B. Undue Influence (BGL 63-86)

- Capacity is the capacity to form testamentary intent free from undue influence
- You must be able to point to a third party and say the will reflects the intent of that third party because they exerted undue influence on the testator

### 1. What Makes Influence "Undue" Rather Than Typical Influence?
- **Susceptibility**
- **Opportunity:** to change the will, or even just to influence the testator's mind
- **Motive:** obvious; people want to influence the will in their favor
- **Causation:** timing; connection of the influence to the outcome
- **Substitution of will:** is it reasonable to say the will, or part of it, reflects the third party's intent rather than the testator's?

### 2. Presumption: Burden Shifting
Showing **(1) a confidential relationship** and **(2) suspicious circumstances** shifts the burden of proof to the proponent to prove the absence of undue influence.

**Confidential relationship: three types**
- **Fiduciary:** legally recognized duty (e.g., principal/agent)
- **Reliant:** testator depends on the person's expertise or good faith (e.g., doctor, lawyer)
- **Dominant-subservient:** testator is unable to care for themselves (e.g., a caregiver, or an adult child living with the testator as a full-time caregiver)

### 3. Cases

**In re Will of Moses (Miss. 1969)**
- Fannie Moses had a 1957 will leaving most of her estate to family; a 1964 will left nearly all of her estate to Clarence Holland
- Holland was Moses's attorney, and the court also found they were having a sexual affair; in combination, these supported an inference of undue influence
- The court seemed to make assumptions about the relationship that likely would not be supported today
  - There was no actual evidence that Holland exerted influence over or caused the 1964 will; the court was reacting to the relationship and essentially assuming it was fraudulent

**Lipper v. Weslow (Tex. Civ. App. 1963)**
- Grandmother has 3 adult children when she dies
  - Instead of leaving her estate equally to all three, she leaves it to only two
  - The child left out is dead and has three children
  - Under typical intestacy, those grandchildren would split their deceased parent's share (1/9 each)
- The will itself stated why she left the grandchildren nothing (though it was drafted in legalese, suggesting her son, an attorney-beneficiary, drafted it; there was no evidence it wasn't her actual intention)
- The grandchildren contested the will. They had standing because a will leaving them out is atypical, and the attorney-drafter, also a beneficiary, aroused suspicion
- The grandchildren provided no evidence of Lipper replacing his mother's intention with his own
- **Holding:** even though the circumstances were suspicious, the grandchildren established no causal connection, and the grandmother's explicit expression of intent to leave them out supported the will's validity. The will was valid; the grandchildren took nothing

**Mere expectancy:** what a **named beneficiary** in the will of a living person has

### 4. Other Approaches to Showing Undue Influence
- Presumption of undue influence in any donative transfer from caregivers
- Special standards for attorney-drafters who are also beneficiaries of the will

## C. Fraud / Duress / Interference (BGL 87-93)

### 1. Fraud
**Elements**
1. Testator deceived by
2. A misrepresentation
3. Made with the intent to deceive the testator; and
4. With the purpose and effect of influencing the testamentary disposition

**Types**
- Fraud in the inducement
- Fraud in the factum

### 2. Duress
A donative transfer is procured by duress if the wrongdoer threatened to perform or did perform a wrongful act that coerced the donor into making a donative transfer the donor would not otherwise have made.

## D. Due Execution (BGL 95-123)

### 1. History of Formality Rules

**Statute of Uses (1536)**
- Abolished uses, where one party held property for the benefit of another and legal title transferred only at the donor's death

**Statute of Wills (1540)**
- Abolition of uses for land led to the creation of a device to pass realty at death: the will. Writing required
- Requirements:
  - Writing
  - Signed by the testator, or by a proxy/agent of the testator
    - At the testator's direction
    - And in the testator's presence (presence = line of sight)
  - Signature must be at the foot or end of the document
  - Signed by the testator, OR the signature is acknowledged by the testator, in the presence of
    - 2 witnesses
    - Present at the same time
  - The same witnesses must
    - Observe; and
    - Sign underneath the testator's or proxy's signature
    - In the presence of the testator (presence = line of sight)
- **[Verify]:** The 1540 Statute of Wills is generally described as requiring only a writing. The detailed list above (signature at the foot, two witnesses present at the same time) looks like a modern attested-will statute. Confirm which statute the professor attached this list to.

**Statute of Frauds (1677)**
- Required all wills to be in writing, signed, and witnessed by 3 people

**Wills Act (1837)**
- Established formal procedures for executing wills, the same for real and personal property

**American law**
- States follow the Statute of Frauds, the Wills Act, or UPC provisions

### 2. UPC Execution Requirements
**What the testator must do**
- Sign, or
- Acknowledge his signature on the will, or
- Acknowledge the will itself, or
- Have another sign the will at his direction and in his conscious presence
- **When:** in the presence of two witnesses

**What the witnesses must do**
- Observe: witness the testator's action (signing or acknowledgment)
- Sign their names as evidence of having witnessed the execution
  - Within a reasonable time after witnessing

### 3. Functions of Formality Requirements
1. **Ritual/cautionary:** signals to the testator that this is real and legal and has an effect; impresses the seriousness of the act
2. **Evidentiary:** evidence that it is the true intention of the testator
3. **Protective:** protects against contest
4. **Channeling:** we want to know a court down the road will recognize the will; it serves as a template so courts everywhere can recognize a valid will

### 4. Not Required but Should ALWAYS Be Included

**Attestation clause**
- Present-tense clause specifically stating that the requirements of a will have been duly followed

**Self-proving affidavit**
- Fuller description of what happened at execution
- An affidavit sworn by the witnesses (in some jurisdictions, the witnesses and the testator)
- In some states (e.g., California), it is incorporated into the attestation clause
- Usually no need to find the witnesses again

### 5. Execution Issues
- **Order of events matters:** witnesses must sign *after* the testator signs or acknowledges
- **Text below the signature, or text that appears to have been added after execution**
  - Where a signature must be at the end/foot of the will, the whole will is invalid
  - If you know it was added afterwards, you ignore it
  - **[Verify]:** The notes attribute the signature-at-the-end rule to the UPC. The UPC (§ 2-502) does not require the signature to be at the end; that rule comes from statutes like the one listed under "Statute of Wills" above. Confirm with class materials.
- **Testator dies while the witnesses are signing but before they finish?** Courts are split

### 6. Cases

**Stevens v. Casdorph**
- Testator went to a bank to execute a will; a teller took it into the back to have two other employees witness and sign it
- No valid will: even though everyone's intentions were clear, that does not replace the formal requirement that the witnesses actually be **present** when the testator signs or acknowledges the will

**Estate of Morea**

## G. Holographic and Conditional Wills (BGL 153-60)

### 1. Holographic Wills
**Three types of statutes**
1. Will must be **entirely** in the testator's handwriting
   - Interpreted three ways: strictly, surplusage, or intent to incorporate
2. **Material provisions** must be in the testator's handwriting
3. ***Material portions*** must be in the testator's handwriting **(current)**
   - Printed material can be used for context
   - *Example:* a will handwritten on Hilton Hotel letterhead is admissible, and the letterhead is used for context

**In re Estate of Kuralt**
- Kuralt had an extramarital relationship with Shannon, was her main financial support, and was close to her and her 3 children
- **1985:** built a cabin on a 20-acre plot with Shannon; in 1987 bought the two adjoining parcels, totaling 90 acres
- **1989 holograph:** "In the event of my death, I bequeath to Patricia Elizabeth Shannon all my interest in land, buildings, furnishings and personal belongings on Burma Road, Twin Bridges, Montana." Mailed a copy to Shannon
- **1994 formal will:** did not name Shannon; left the estate to his wife *(the opinion says his wife and their two children)*
- **April 1997:** deeded the 20-acre parcel (with the cabin) to Shannon in a disguised "sale" (Kuralt supplied the purchase money)
- **June 18, 1997:** the day he entered the hospital, he wrote to Shannon: "I'll have the lawyer visit the hospital to be sure you inherit the rest of the place in MT. if it comes to that." Signed "C." Enclosed checks for $8,000 and $9,000. He never called the lawyer and died unexpectedly
- Shannon argued the 1997 letter was a codicil; the Estate argued it expressed only a future intent to make a will

*Did the letter express present testamentary intent?* Yes:
- Long, close relationship with Shannon, including the checks in the letter
- The 20-acre parcel was transferred without real consideration, and extrinsic evidence showed intent to transfer the rest
- He wrote the letter "in extremis" (facing death) and died two weeks later
- Avoided the lawyer to keep the relationship secret
- He underlined "inherit," showing a posthumous disposition

*Was it a codicil to the 1994 will?* Yes:
- The Estate argued a codicil must refer to the prior will or itself be a valid will
- The court held the letter met the threshold requirements for a valid holographic will and was "a codicil as a matter of law"
- Because it made a specific bequest and did not dispose of the whole estate. The court quoted the Official Comments to MCA § 72-2-527: "when the second will does not make a complete disposition of the testator's estate, the second will is more in the nature of a codicil to the first will."
- The Estate had the same opportunity to argue the point in the district court as on appeal

### 2. Conditional Wills
- The language of a condition is presumed to be language of inducement, unless clearly intended as a condition on the will's operative effect

### 3. Nuncupative (Oral) Wills
- Wills generally must be in writing
  - Some jurisdictions have exceptions with requirements attached, oral wills being one
- The goal is to avoid fraud
  - In most states, and practically, oral wills have such strict requirements that they are virtually never valid
